# ASD-STE100 Issue 9: Compact Agent Guide

This reference distills the operational content of ASD-STE100 Issue 9
(2025-01-15) for an agent that must draft, rewrite, or review text. Read this
file completely before you produce STE content.

This guide supports practical application. It does not replace the official
standard for certification, contractual interpretation, or an exact ruling on
an unusual word. For the official standard and its usage limits, see
[ASD-STE100.md](ASD-STE100.md).

This is an independently written, unofficial aid. ASD and the STEMG do not
endorse, certify, or approve it.

## 1. Operating method

Use this sequence for each task.

1. Identify the required facts, actions, conditions, warnings, labels,
   identifiers, measurements, and fixed text.
2. Classify each passage as procedural, descriptive, or mixed.
3. Make a terminology list. Select one technical noun for each item and one
   verb for each action.
4. Draft with approved vocabulary and permitted technical terms.
5. Apply the rules for noun phrases, verbs, sentences, paragraphs, safety
   instructions, and punctuation.
6. Count the words in each sentence.
7. Check each ordinary word for its approved part of speech and meaning.
8. Check each non-dictionary noun or verb against the applicable technical-term
   category.
9. Compare the result with the source. Restore any fact, condition, sequence,
   safety result, or degree of obligation that changed.
10. Do the final compliance pass in section 15.

Preserve these elements exactly unless the user requests a change:

- code, commands, paths, URLs, API names, and identifiers;
- part numbers, model names, product names, and interface labels;
- values, units, tolerances, formulas, and mathematical symbols;
- quoted text, legal text, and required terminology;
- the level of obligation in requirements;
- the cause, consequence, and severity of a safety instruction.

Treat literal fixed text as quoted or technical text. Do not silently rewrite a
button label, command, code token, quotation, or legal phrase.

When the requested content is outside the usual technical-documentation
domain, apply the same controlled-language rules as far as the subject permits.
Proper names and valid subject terms can be technical nouns. State a material
constraint only when strict STE would remove or change necessary meaning.

## 2. Vocabulary rules

### 2.1 Permitted word classes

Use only:

- an approved dictionary word;
- an applicable technical noun;
- an applicable technical verb.

An uppercase dictionary headword is approved. A lowercase headword is not
approved. Alternatives shown for a lowercase entry are suggestions; the
sentence frequently needs a different construction.

For an approved word:

- use only the listed part of speech;
- use only the listed meaning;
- use only the listed grammatical forms;
- obey any help or context restriction in its entry.

Approval of a spelling does not approve all common meanings of that spelling.
For example, `ABOUT` is approved as a preposition that means "concerned with."
Use `APPROXIMATELY` for quantity and `AROUND` for rotation or position.

Plural forms of approved countable nouns are permitted unless an entry says
otherwise. Comparative and superlative adjective forms are permitted only as
specified. For adjectives that use `MORE` and `MOST`, those forms do not need
separate entries.

Use American English spelling unless an official project directive requires a
different spelling. Keep the spelling of quoted text unchanged.

### 2.2 Vocabulary decision

For each content word, use this decision:

1. Is the word approved for this part of speech and meaning? Use it.
2. Is the word an applicable technical noun? Use it as a noun or noun modifier.
3. Is the word an applicable technical verb? Use it as a verb.
4. Is an approved alternative accurate? Change the construction and use it.
5. If none applies, replace the concept with a short, accurate construction
   made from approved words.
6. If replacement changes required meaning, keep the fixed term and identify
   the constraint.

Prefer the most specific approved word that preserves the meaning. Do not use a
vague technical term to bypass the controlled vocabulary.

## 3. Technical nouns

A technical noun names a specified concept in a subject field. It can contain a
word that is not approved in the dictionary. Use it only as a noun or as a noun
modifier.

Use technical nouns from these 22 categories:

1. Official parts information: design items, assemblies, components, and parts.
2. Vehicles or machines and locations on them.
3. Tools and support equipment, their parts, and their locations.
4. Materials, consumables, contaminants, and unwanted material.
5. Facilities, infrastructure, logistics, storage, and transport procedures.
6. Systems, components, circuits, functions, configurations, and parts.
7. Mathematical, scientific, and engineering terms and formulas.
8. Navigation and geographic terms.
9. Numbers, units of measurement, time, and their symbols.
10. Quoted text that cannot change, such as labels, signs, and display text.
11. Professional roles, persons, groups, organizations, and geopolitical
    entities.
12. Parts of the body.
13. Common personal items, food, and beverages.
14. Medical terms.
15. Official documents, document parts, standards, specifications, and
    guidelines.
16. Environmental and operational conditions.
17. Colors. Treat colors as technical nouns; do not use comparative or
    superlative color forms.
18. Damage, defect, degradation, and malfunction terms.
19. Computer science, information technology, communication, and interface
    terms.
20. Civil and military operations, service delivery, support, and life-cycle
    terms.
21. Law, regulations, contracts, certificates, standards, and legal papers.
22. Animals, plants, and other life forms in technical or environmental
    contexts.

Apply these controls:

- Prefer a technical noun already approved by the company, industry, customer,
  or subject field.
- If no approved term exists, select a term that is short and easy to
  understand.
- Keep a new multi-word technical noun to three words when possible.
- Do not use regional vocabulary, slang, jargon, or an informal specialist
  nickname.
- Use the same technical noun for the same item throughout the text.
- Do not use a technical noun as a verb.
- A word can be both a technical noun and a technical verb only when its use
  independently qualifies for both categories.

Examples:

- Write `APPLY OIL TO THE SURFACES.` Do not use `oil` as a verb.
- Write `PUT CLAMPS ON THE CABLE.` Do not use `clamp` as a verb when the
  approved noun construction is sufficient.
- A software `backup` or `backup file` can be a category 19 technical noun.
- A named `home button` can be an approved company or product technical noun.

## 4. Technical verbs

A technical verb identifies a specified process in a subject field. It must
obey the same tense, form, voice, and sentence rules as an approved verb.

Use technical verbs only in these categories:

1. Manufacturing processes:
   - remove material, such as drill, grind, mill, ream, or unsolder;
   - add material, such as insulate or remetal;
   - attach material, such as braze, crimp, solder, or weld;
   - change strength, structure, or physical properties;
   - change a surface finish;
   - change the shape of a material.
2. Computer processes and applications:
   - input and output;
   - user-interface and application operations;
   - system operations.
3. Instructions and information for an applicable subject field:
   - engineering, mathematics, and science;
   - medicine;
   - civil and military operations;
   - navigation;
   - automotive and railway;
   - energy, oil, and gas.
4. Law and regulations, only in legal or regulatory text.

Use an approved dictionary verb when it gives the meaning accurately. Use a
technical verb when the approved vocabulary cannot name the required process
with sufficient precision.

Context controls the permission. `ENTER` can be a technical verb for data input:
`ENTER YOUR PASSWORD.` For movement into an area, write `GO INTO THE AREA.`

Do not use a broad process verb when a precise process verb is available. For
example, use `REAM THE HOLE` when reaming is the specified operation.

Do not use a technical verb as a noun. A permitted past participle can modify a
technical noun, such as `REAMED HOLE`.

## 5. Multi-word nouns

Keep a multi-word noun to a maximum of three words. A long stack of nouns and
adjectives is difficult to parse and can have more than one meaning.

Use these methods:

1. Use an established short form after the full term is identified.
2. Remove words that do not identify the item.
3. Use an index number, figure reference, or context to avoid repeated detail.
4. Split information into a noun plus a separate descriptive clause.
5. Use hyphens to show words that operate as one unit when the relationship
   would otherwise be unclear.

Keep approved technical names intact when shortening would create ambiguity.
Count a recognized name or fixed technical term according to the word-count
rules in section 11.

Examples:

- Prefer `fuel pump cover` to a longer modifier stack.
- Prefer `the screws (10)` after the illustration identifies the screw type.
- Write `the cable that supplies power` when a long noun stack would be
  ambiguous.

## 6. Verb forms and voice

### 6.1 Permitted forms and tenses

Use only verb forms given in the dictionary or valid forms of an applicable
technical verb.

Use:

- the infinitive;
- the imperative;
- the simple present;
- the simple past;
- the simple future with `WILL`;
- a permitted past participle as an adjective.

Do not use:

- present perfect, past perfect, or future perfect;
- progressive or continuous tenses;
- complex modal constructions;
- unlisted participles;
- an `-ing` form as an ordinary verb form.

An `-ing` form is permitted only when it is an established technical noun or a
modifier in a technical noun, such as a named process or part.

Rewrite complex tense:

- `The valve has closed.` becomes `The valve is closed.` when the state is
  important.
- `The technician has completed the test.` becomes `The technician completed
  the test.`
- `The pump is operating.` becomes `The pump operates.`
- `After tightening the nut, ...` becomes `After you tighten the nut, ...`

Do not use auxiliary verbs to build a complex construction. Use the simplest
approved tense that preserves the time relationship.

### 6.2 Active voice

Use active voice.

In descriptive writing, passive voice is permitted only when the agent is
unknown. In procedures, write a command and omit the understood agent:
`REMOVE THE COVER.`

To remove passive voice:

- name the agent: `THE PUMP SUPPLIES THE FLUID`;
- use an imperative: `INSTALL THE SEAL`;
- describe the state with a permitted adjective: `THE VALVE IS CLOSED`;
- change the sentence so the actual subject performs the action.

Do not invent an agent. If the agent is unknown and relevant, a short passive
sentence can be the clearest accurate construction.

### 6.3 Name actions with verbs

Use a verb to name an action. Avoid a noun phrase that hides the action.

- Prefer `ADJUST THE VALVE` to `DO AN ADJUSTMENT OF THE VALVE`.
- Prefer `EXAMINE THE SURFACE` to `DO AN EXAMINATION OF THE SURFACE`.
- Use an approved procedural noun when the noun identifies a defined task:
  `DO A LEAK TEST`.

## 7. General sentence construction

Write short, direct sentences with a clear subject and verb.

Apply these rules:

- Do not omit necessary words.
- Do not use contractions.
- Keep the grammatical relationship between words clear.
- Put modifiers next to the words they modify.
- State the subject again when a pronoun can refer to more than one noun.
- Use articles (`a`, `an`, `the`) and demonstratives (`this`, `these`) when
  applicable.
- Do not use a definite article before a noun that is immediately followed by
  an alphanumeric identifier. Write `TAG CIRCUIT BREAKER 36L7.`
- Use a vertical list for complex information.
- Use connecting words and phrases to show a clear relationship between
  related sentences.

Do not compress text by deleting articles, subjects, auxiliary words, or
objects. Shortness must come from a simpler construction and fewer ideas.

Use a vertical list when a sentence is long or complex because it contains many
items or actions.

Apply these list rules:

- Put a colon at the end of the introductory sentence.
- Identify each item with a number, letter, dash, bullet, or other permitted
  symbol.
- Start each item with an uppercase letter.
- Use an article before the subject noun when applicable.
- Put a period after each full-sentence item.
- Do not put a period after a non-sentence item, except after the last item.
- Do not put a comma or semicolon at the end of an item.
- Make each item connect clearly and grammatically to the introduction.
- Keep items at the same list level when possible.
- Do not mix procedural and descriptive items in the same list.

Make list items grammatically parallel. In a safety list, repeat `DO NOT` for
each applicable negative command.

## 8. Procedural writing

Procedural text tells the reader what to do.

### 8.1 Sentences

- Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.
- Give one instruction in each sentence.
- Two or more actions can share a sentence only when they occur at the same
  time or are a single coordinated operation.
- Use the imperative form for instructions.
- Put a necessary prior condition before the command, followed by a comma.

Examples:

- `REMOVE THE COVER.`
- `IF THE LIGHT IS ON, STOP THE TEST.`
- `HOLD THE LEVER AND TURN THE NUT.` This is permitted only when the two
  actions occur at the same time.
- Split sequential actions: `OPEN THE VALVE. THEN, START THE PUMP.`

Do not use `should`, `shall`, `may`, `might`, or a passive requirement when a
direct command is required.

Use `MUST` for a mandatory descriptive requirement. Use an imperative for a
task step.

### 8.2 Conditions

Place a condition first when the reader must know it before the action:

`IF THE PRESSURE IS MORE THAN 10 BAR, CLOSE THE VALVE.`

Use `WHEN` for an expected event or state. Use `IF` for a condition that can or
cannot occur. Keep each condition attached to the command that it controls.

### 8.3 Descriptive statements in procedures

A procedure can include a short descriptive statement when the reader needs
information to do the task correctly. Keep it separate from the command unless
it is a prior condition.

### 8.4 Notes

Use a note only for information. A note can identify a permitted alternative.
Do not put an instruction, requirement, limitation, or warning in a note. Move
any action out of the note and make it a step. Move any hazard into a safety
instruction.

## 9. Descriptive writing

Descriptive text explains a system, condition, result, rule, or relationship.

### 9.1 Information structure

- Give information gradually.
- Introduce the subject before details about the subject.
- Move from general information to specific information.
- Use key words and key phrases to make the logical structure visible.
- Repeat a key technical noun when repetition prevents ambiguity.

Avoid elegant variation. Repetition of the same approved term is useful in
controlled technical text.

### 9.2 Sentences and paragraphs

- Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.
- Give each sentence one subject.
- Use a paragraph for a group of related sentences.
- Give each paragraph one topic.
- Start each paragraph with its topic sentence.
- Use no more than six sentences in a paragraph.

Start a new paragraph when the topic, condition, time, location, system, or
purpose changes.

Use active voice. Use passive voice only when the agent is unknown.

## 10. Safety instructions

A safety instruction must identify the risk and tell the reader how to prevent
or control it.

Use the risk level required by the governing document or organization. Common
labels include `DANGER`, `WARNING`, and `CAUTION`. Make sure that the label
agrees with an accurate risk analysis. For example, use `WARNING` when the
identified risk can cause injury or death. Do not weaken an accurate risk level
during a rewrite. If fixed source text has an apparently incorrect label,
identify the conflict instead of silently changing the fixed text.

Write a safety instruction in this order:

1. Start with a clear command or condition.
2. State the hazard or unsafe condition.
3. When the result is known, state the possible result or consequence.

Keep the cause-and-effect relationship explicit.
Use a maximum of 20 words in each safety-instruction sentence. Do not invent a
consequence that the source or an applicable authority does not supply. If a
necessary consequence is missing, identify the missing safety information.

Example pattern:

`WARNING: DISCONNECT THE POWER SUPPLY BEFORE YOU OPEN THE UNIT. HIGH VOLTAGE
CAN KILL YOU.`

Do not:

- hide the required action in a descriptive sentence;
- use vague language such as `BE CAREFUL` when a specific action is available;
- omit a known possible result;
- weaken `will` or `can` into an uncertain expression without source support;
- combine unrelated hazards in one safety instruction.

Preserve safety terminology, exposure routes, thresholds, protective
equipment, time limits, and first-aid actions.

## 11. Punctuation and word count

### 11.1 Punctuation

Use standard English punctuation, except the semicolon. Do not use a semicolon.

Use:

- a period to end a sentence;
- a comma to divide a prior condition from its command;
- a colon to introduce a vertical list or explanation;
- parentheses only for the permitted functions below;
- hyphens to connect words that are directly related.

Use parentheses to:

- refer to an illustration or text;
- include an item identifier;
- identify a procedural step;
- include an abbreviation;
- give singular and plural forms together;
- explain a word or part of a sentence;
- include an alternative.

Do not put essential safety meaning or a required action only in parentheses.

### 11.2 Counting words

The procedural and safety-instruction limit is 20 words. The descriptive limit
is 25 words.

Apply these counting rules:

- In a vertical list, a colon has the effect of a period for word count.
- Parenthetical text counts as one word in its containing sentence.
- A hyphenated word counts as one word.
- Count each of these as one word:
  - a number;
  - a number with its unit;
  - an abbreviation;
  - an alphanumeric identifier;
  - quoted text;
  - a title or heading;
  - text on a placard or label;
  - a proper name of a person, group, organization, or geopolitical entity.

Word-count permission does not make a sentence clear. Split a sentence that is
grammatically dense even when it is below the limit.

## 12. Writing practices

### 12.1 Change the construction

A word-for-word substitution frequently produces incorrect or unnatural text.
Change the sentence structure when necessary.

Use these methods:

- change a nominalization to a verb;
- change passive voice to active voice;
- replace a phrasal verb with one approved verb;
- split one sentence into two;
- replace a vague modifier with a condition or an accurate value;
- change a negative statement to a direct positive instruction when meaning
  permits;
- replace an unapproved connector with two short sentences and an approved
  connector.

### 12.2 Use approved words correctly

An approved word can still be wrong when:

- it has an unapproved part of speech;
- it has an unapproved meaning;
- it is in an unapproved verb form;
- it forms a prohibited phrasal verb;
- it is too vague for the context;
- the dictionary help limits it to a different context.

### 12.3 Avoid phrasal verbs

Do not make a phrasal verb by combining a verb with an adverb or preposition
when the combination has an idiomatic meaning. Use one approved verb.

Common replacements:

- `carry out` -> `DO`;
- `find out` -> `FIND`;
- `go on` -> `CONTINUE`;
- `put back` -> `INSTALL` or `PUT ... BACK`, according to the literal meaning;
- `take off` -> `REMOVE`, except when `take off` is an approved navigation
  technical verb;
- `turn off` -> `STOP`, `DEACTIVATE`, `DE-ENERGIZE`, or `SET ... TO OFF`,
  according to the system.

Some multi-word approved verbs, such as `COME ON`, `GO OFF`, `MAKE SURE`, and
`PUT ON`, are permitted as listed. Do not infer permission for similar phrases.

### 12.4 Maintain a consistent style

- Use one term for one concept.
- Use the same sentence pattern for repeated operations.
- Keep warning labels and requirement words consistent.
- Keep capitalization, units, abbreviations, and references consistent.
- Keep list items parallel.

### 12.5 General recommendations

Use `THAT` when it makes a subordinate clause clear. Do not omit it when the
omission can cause the reader to group words incorrectly.

Use `WITH` carefully. It can mean accompaniment, possession, instrument, or
condition. Rewrite the sentence when more than one relationship is possible.

Use pronouns only when the antecedent is clear. Repeat the noun when `it`,
`they`, `this`, or `these` can refer to more than one thing.

Do not use `THIS` alone when the noun is needed for clarity. Prefer `THIS
PROCEDURE`, `THIS CONDITION`, or `THIS VALUE`.

Be careful with false friends in international English. Select words by their
approved English meaning.

Avoid Latin abbreviations when an approved English phrase is clearer. Use
`FOR EXAMPLE` instead of `e.g.` and `THAT IS` instead of `i.e.` unless the
abbreviation is fixed text.

Use inclusive language. Use a role, `PERSON`, `PERSONNEL`, or a clear plural
construction instead of an unnecessary gendered term.

Use the possessive form only when the relationship is clear. Prefer an `OF`
construction when the possessive can identify ownership, location, or part
relationship ambiguously.

### 13.3 High-risk common words

Check these words carefully because their everyday use is often broader than
their approved use:

- `ABOUT`: concerned with only.
- `ABOVE` and `BELOW`: physical position; use `MORE THAN` or `LESS THAN` for
  values.
- `AFTER` and `BEFORE`: keep the sequence explicit.
- `AS`: approved as a preposition; use `BECAUSE` for cause.
- `BACK`: approved as an adverb for return to an initial condition.
- `BAD` and `GOOD`: permitted, but use a more accurate word when available.
- `BY`: use for mathematical functions; use a clear active construction for
  agency.
- `CASE`: use `CONDITION` for a state and `IF` for a condition.
- `CHECK`: approved as a noun. Select `MAKE SURE`, `EXAMINE`, or `MEASURE` for
  the intended action.
- `COMPLETE`: approved as a verb. Use `COMPLETED`, `FULL`, or `ALL` for the
  applicable adjective meaning.
- `CONTROL`: make sure the subject truly sends signals or keeps a value within
  a limit.
- `DIRECTLY`: use only for direct procedure or effect.
- `EACH`: identifies members separately. Use `ALL` for the group.
- `EFFECT`: approved as a noun; do not use it as a verb.
- `FINAL`: use `LAST`.
- `FREE`: use for freedom of movement; avoid invented compounds such as
  `dust-free`.
- `GREAT`: use `LARGE`, `MORE THAN`, or `VERY`.
- `IN`: do not use it when `INTO` is necessary for movement.
- `LIKE`: it is not an approved dictionary word. Use `FOR EXAMPLE` to introduce
  an example, or use a valid technical term when the context permits it.
- `OVER`: select the exact relation.
- `RIGHT`: use only for direction when that is the approved meaning; use
  `CORRECT` for correctness.
- `SINCE`: use for time only; use `BECAUSE` for cause.
- `THROUGH`: distinguish movement, time, and means.
- `UNDER`: select `BELOW`, `IN`, or `LESS THAN`.
- `WHILE`: use for simultaneous time; do not use it as a contrast connector.

For an exact meaning or an unlisted word, search the complete dictionary entry
in an official copy of ASD-STE100 Issue 9.

## 14. Rewrite patterns

Use these patterns as transformations. Preserve the original degree of
obligation and technical meaning.

### Nominalization to verb

Non-STE:

`Do an inspection of the cable and a verification of the connector position.`

STE:

`EXAMINE THE CABLE. MAKE SURE THAT THE CONNECTOR IS IN THE CORRECT POSITION.`

### Passive procedure to imperative

Non-STE:

`The cover should be removed before the unit is tested.`

STE:

`REMOVE THE COVER. THEN, DO A TEST OF THE UNIT.`

### Complex tense to simple tense

Non-STE:

`The system has been operating incorrectly.`

STE:

`THE SYSTEM OPERATED INCORRECTLY.`

Use a different simple tense if the source describes the current state.

### Long condition to two sentences

Non-STE:

`If the pressure, which can increase when the temperature is high, is above
the permitted limit, the operator should immediately stop the test.`

STE:

`A HIGH TEMPERATURE CAN INCREASE THE PRESSURE. IF THE PRESSURE IS MORE THAN
THE PERMITTED LIMIT, STOP THE TEST IMMEDIATELY.`

### Ambiguous pronoun to repeated noun

Non-STE:

`Remove the valve from the pump and examine it.`

STE, when the valve must be examined:

`REMOVE THE VALVE FROM THE PUMP. EXAMINE THE VALVE.`

### Long noun stack to clause

Non-STE:

`Install the emergency power supply control panel access cover.`

STE:

`INSTALL THE ACCESS COVER FOR THE EMERGENCY POWER CONTROL PANEL.`

Verify that the revised noun relationships match the equipment design.

### Vague amount to value

Non-STE:

`Apply a small amount of oil.`

STE:

`APPLY 5 ML OF OIL.`

Use a value only when the source or an authority supplies it. Otherwise, keep
the most accurate permitted qualitative term.

### Phrasal verb to approved verb

Non-STE:

`Carry out the test and find out the cause of the fault.`

STE:

`DO THE TEST. FIND THE CAUSE OF THE FAULT.`

### Instruction hidden in a note

Non-STE:

`NOTE: The technician should install the pin before the test.`

STE:

`INSTALL THE PIN BEFORE THE TEST.`

### Safety instruction

Non-STE:

`Be careful when opening the unit because dangerous voltage may be present.`

STE:

`WARNING: DISCONNECT THE POWER SUPPLY BEFORE YOU OPEN THE UNIT. HIGH VOLTAGE
CAN KILL YOU.`

Use the source risk level and consequence.

## 15. Final compliance pass

Do this pass after the rewrite is complete. A fresh subagent that did not write
the candidate text must do the pass. Use a context-free spawn when the platform
supports it. If an independent context is not possible, the review is
incomplete. The subagent does only this reviewer role and does not start
another subagent.

Give the reviewer:

- the complete source text;
- the complete candidate text;
- the passage classification and unverified candidate terminology list;
- this complete guide;
- the official Issue 9 PDF or its local path when it is available.

Do not give the reviewer the writer's conclusions, suspected errors, or
expected corrections. Do not give it inherited conversation context when a
context-free spawn is available. The reviewer must independently examine the
candidate and validate each entry in the candidate terminology list.

### Reviewer method

1. Compare the candidate with the source for meaning and scope.
2. Examine every word occurrence in the candidate in order and in context. Do
   not use a sample, search only for familiar problem words, or rely on spelling
   alone.
3. For each ordinary word, verify its approved part of speech, meaning, form,
   and applicable dictionary help.
4. For each word that is not an approved ordinary word, verify that it is one
   of these permitted exceptions:
   - a technical noun in a named category;
   - a technical verb in a named category;
   - exact fixed or quoted text.
5. Propose a rewrite or removal for each unapproved word. Rewrite the sentence
   when a direct word substitution is not accurate or natural.
6. Use the complete official dictionary entry for each word that this guide
   does not resolve. Mark the word as unresolved when the entry is not
   available or does not give a clear decision.
7. Apply the remaining checks in this section.
8. If any wording must change, return `CHANGES_REQUIRED`. Do not approve text
   that you changed or proposed.

The reviewer must return:

- the exact complete candidate that it examined;
- the exact first line, exact last line, line count, sentence count, and total
  word-occurrence count;
- an ordered occurrence ledger with one row for every word occurrence, with
  contiguous numbers from 1 through the total count;
- each ledger row's exact word, sentence or line location, grammatical use,
  decision, and evidence;
- a finding and proposed correction for each rejected or uncertain word;
- a permission record for each retained word that is not an approved ordinary
  word;
- all unresolved words or exact lookups that were not possible;
- a clear `PASS`, `CHANGES_REQUIRED`, or `INCOMPLETE` result.

For this review ledger, count each lexical token in the prose. Count each word
inside quoted text, interface labels, and multi-word terms. Count a hyphenated
word, number with its unit, URL, path, command, code token, or identifier as one
token. Remove attached punctuation from the recorded token. This audit count
is separate from the sentence word count in Section 11.

For an approved ordinary word, the evidence must identify the approved
headword, part of speech, meaning, and form that permit the exact use.

For a technical noun or technical verb, the permission record must identify:

- the complete term and every occurrence;
- its grammatical use;
- the named category;
- the source, project terminology, or other authority that establishes the
  term.

For a new technical noun, explain why the term satisfies Section 3. For a
technical verb, also explain why approved vocabulary does not give the
necessary precise meaning.

For fixed or quoted text, the permission record must identify the source
location and confirm an exact match.

A pass means all these conditions are true:

- The reviewer made and proposed no textual change.
- The exact candidate, boundary records, and counts agree.
- The occurrence ledger covers every word in order with no gap or duplicate
  number.
- No unapproved ordinary word or unresolved word remains.
- Every technical-term and fixed-text exception has the required evidence.

A statement such as “looks compliant” without the required examination and
records is not a pass.

The primary agent must verify the ledger sequence, counts, boundary records,
and permission records. It must resolve all findings and compare each proposed
correction with the source. After any wording changes, a different fresh
subagent must examine the complete revised candidate. The exact unchanged text
from the last successful pass is the text that can be given to the user.

If the review is incomplete, give only the incomplete status and blocker. Do
not give candidate text, partial rewritten text, excerpts, or suggested
wording.

### Meaning and scope

- All source facts are present.
- All steps remain in the correct sequence.
- Each condition controls the correct action.
- Requirement strength is unchanged.
- Safety causes and consequences are unchanged.
- Fixed text, identifiers, values, units, and tolerances are exact.

### Vocabulary

- Each ordinary word is approved for its part of speech and meaning.
- Each verb uses an approved form.
- Each non-dictionary noun fits a technical-noun category.
- Each non-dictionary verb fits a technical-verb category.
- No technical noun is incorrectly used as a verb.
- No technical verb is incorrectly used as a noun.
- One term identifies one concept.
- American spelling is used outside fixed text.

### Grammar and structure

- Procedural sentences use the imperative.
- Procedural sentences have no more than 20 words.
- Safety-instruction sentences have no more than 20 words.
- Descriptive sentences have no more than 25 words.
- Each descriptive sentence has one subject.
- Each sentence has a clear subject and verb, except a direct imperative.
- Each procedural sentence has one instruction unless actions are simultaneous.
- Active voice is used; permitted passive voice has an unknown agent.
- No complex tense is present.
- No unapproved `-ing` verb form is present.
- Articles and demonstratives are present when applicable.
- Pronoun references are unambiguous.
- Multi-word nouns have no more than three words when possible.
- Each paragraph starts with a topic sentence.
- Paragraphs contain one topic and no more than six sentences.

### Presentation

- No semicolon is present.
- Conditions have correct punctuation.
- Lists are parallel and easy to scan.
- Parentheses have a permitted function.
- Hyphens show direct word relationships.
- Notes contain information only.
- Safety instructions have a risk label and a command or condition.
- Safety instructions state a known possible result and do not invent one.

### Output claims

Do not claim certification, official approval, or guaranteed compliance.
Describe the result as written or reviewed with ASD-STE100 Issue 9 guidance.
